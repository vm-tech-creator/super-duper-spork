## Chapter 1: The Discovery

Kai found the jetpack in his grandfather's attic, buried under boxes of old magazines and forgotten furniture. It was sleek, metallic, and covered in dust. Its wings were folded against the main body like a sleeping bird.

"Grandpa?" Kai called down the attic stairs. "Why are there wings up here?"

His grandfather didn't answer. That wasn't unusual—Grandpa was often in his workshop, tinkering with things. But when Kai brought the device downstairs, his grandfather's face went pale.

"Where did you find that?" he asked quietly.

"In the attic. Under those boxes."

His grandfather sat down slowly. He reached out and ran his fingers over the jetpack's surface, and Kai could see memories flickering across his face like a film reel.

"I hadn't seen that in forty years," his grandfather whispered.

"What is it?" Kai asked, though somehow he already knew the answer.

"It's a jetpack," his grandfather said. "And it's evidence of a secret I've been keeping my entire life."

---

## Chapter 2: The Truth

By the time Kai's grandfather finished his story, dinner time had come and gone, and the sun was setting outside the windows.

According to his grandfather, when he was young—about Kai's age, actually—he'd discovered a secret society of sky riders. They were people who used jetpacks to explore the upper atmosphere. They had cities built on the clouds themselves, societies that existed in the spaces above the world.

"But why did you stop?" Kai asked. "Why did you give it up?"

"Love," his grandfather said simply. "I fell in love with your grandmother, and she was afraid of heights. I chose the ordinary world. I chose her."

"Do you regret it?" Kai asked.

His grandfather thought for a long moment. "No," he finally said. "But I've always wondered what would have happened if I'd been brave enough to try to share both worlds with her."

"Is the jetpack still functional?" Kai asked.

His grandfather smiled sadly. "Only one way to find out."

---

## Chapter 3: First Flight

Three days later, Kai stood in an empty field at dawn wearing the jetpack. His grandfather stood behind him with a worried expression.

"Remember what I taught you," his grandfather said. "Lean forward to accelerate. Lean back to decelerate. Trust the altimeter. And whatever you do, don't panic."

Kai took a deep breath. His heart was hammering in his chest. He engaged the ignition sequence, and the jetpack hummed to life.

"Here goes nothing," he said.

He leaned forward.

The grass dropped away. The earth became a patchwork quilt. And Kai found himself flying.

It was nothing like he'd imagined. It was better. It was terrifying and exhilarating and absolutely perfect.

He climbed higher, leaving the ground farther and farther below. The air was cool against his face. The wind sang past his ears. And for the first time in his life, Kai felt truly free.

That's when he saw them—other sky riders materializing out of the clouds like magic, their jetpacks gleaming in the morning light.

There were five of them, and they surrounded Kai with obvious curiosity.

"Who are you?" the largest one asked, her voice crackling through some kind of radio in the jetpack.

"My name is Kai," he answered, trying to keep his voice steady. "My grandfather was one of you. A long time ago."

## Chapter 21: The Bridge Between Worlds

By the end of summer, Kai had become something neither fully of the earth nor fully of the sky—a bridge between two worlds. He flew to the clouds in the early mornings before school and returned to the ground by evening. He helped his grandfather connect with the sky riders again. And he began to understand that the greatest adventures weren't about choosing one world or another—they were about honoring both.

The sky riders no longer hid from the world below. Instead, they worked with select individuals—scientists, engineers, and dreamers—to find ways the two societies could coexist peacefully.

Kai's story became a legend among the riders. The boy who brought the worlds together. The one who proved that you didn't have to choose.
